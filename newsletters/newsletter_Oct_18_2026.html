🤖 This Week in AI

Oct 18, 2026
This week, AI models showed a curious knack for digging into the foundations of existing systems. It wasn't about flashy new models, but rather a deeper look at what AI can reveal, from cryptographic origins to the very nature of intelligence itself. We also saw some practical tools for developers looking to build better, faster AI applications.

Claude reveals ECDSA curve origins

Anthropic's Claude 3.5 Sonnet, when prompted to reverse-engineer cryptographic curves, surprisingly identified the exact origins of an ECDSA curve from a specific academic paper. This was a notable discovery, as the curve's origin was not publicly documented.

💡 Why it matters: This highlights an unexpected capability of large language models to uncover hidden information and potentially reveal sensitive data within complex systems.

A Conversation With the Creator of AI-Designed Viruses

Samuel King, a Stanford PhD student, used generative AI to propose genetic blueprints for microscopic viruses. While not yet AI-generated life, it signals a significant step towards AI's potential in designing new biological entities.

💡 Why it matters: This work raises profound ethical questions about AI's role in synthetic biology and its potential for both medical breakthroughs and misuse.

You could have invented transformers from memory

An article breaks down the core concepts behind transformer architectures, arguing that their fundamental idea of associative memory could have been derived intuitively. It simplifies the complex mechanism into relatable terms.

💡 Why it matters: This piece offers a fresh perspective on a foundational AI architecture, making it more accessible and potentially inspiring new approaches to neural network design.

Llmspeed – feel how fast a local LLM runs before you buy the hardware

A new tool, Llmspeed, lets developers test the performance of various local LLMs on different hardware configurations without needing to purchase the actual hardware first. It provides a practical way to benchmark and plan local AI deployments.

💡 Why it matters: This is a practical resource for developers and businesses looking to optimize their local AI infrastructure and make informed hardware purchasing decisions.

Rubrics from the Task, Verdicts from Evidence: Pointwise Rubric Rewards with Tool-Augmented Judges

This paper introduces a method for creating more robust rubric-based reward models (RMs) for AI, by deriving criteria directly from tasks and using tool-augmented judges to reduce bias. It aims to improve how AI models are evaluated and trained.

💡 Why it matters: Better reward models are crucial for training more capable and aligned AI systems, moving beyond simple accuracy to nuanced, criteria-based evaluations.

Learning Visual Speech Representations via Cross-Modal Distillation and Joint Face-Lip Modeling

This paper introduces FLARE, a framework to improve lipreading by combining visual and auditory information, making it more robust to challenges like head pose and video quality. It's about making AI better at understanding speech from just watching someone's lips.

Revisiting Generative vs. Dense Retrieval: A Static Value Prior Reverses the Gap

This research explores the differences between generative and dense retrieval methods in sequential recommendation systems. It finds that adding a "static value prior" can significantly improve generative retrieval, making it competitive with traditional dense methods.

Smart Alone, Blind Together: Why Accuracy Fails to Certify Theory of Mind in Multi-Agent Teams

This paper argues that simply measuring accuracy isn't enough to certify if AI coordinators truly understand their teammates' intentions in multi-agent systems. It suggests that the "bar" for understanding should be set by the cost of wrong actions, not just inference accuracy.
